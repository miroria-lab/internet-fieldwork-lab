A Clean Secondhand Shop, Wooden Bins, and Almost No Reviews

I spent 25 minutes exploring shops around Antananarivo, Madagascar, through Google Maps from Japan.

I began at a craft market expecting unfamiliar objects. What surprised me more was how ordinary products were transformed by local materials, display methods and packaging.

1. Almost anything can become a souvenir

One shop contained brightly woven bags, large ammonite fossils, paintings of baobab trees, lizard-shaped decorations and spices arranged in circular packages.

The common thread was not one material or one product category. It was the ability to turn plants, fossils, animals and food into objects that immediately looked giftable.

See the craft shop on Google Maps

2. Then I searched for ordinary clothing shops

French searches such as boutique de vêtements initially produced surprisingly little. Changing the term to friperie — a secondhand clothing shop — revealed a different layer of the city.

The shops existed. Many simply had very few reviews or photographs. A living city and its digital map seemed to have very different densities.

3. A neat shop where clothes are piled into wooden bins

Kilobika looked cleaner and more carefully designed than I expected: polished floors, bright lighting, clothing racks and mannequins.

But much of the stock appeared to sit in large wooden bins. Instead of every garment being individually presented, customers seemed invited to search through the piles themselves.

See Kilobika on Google Maps

Why import other countries' old clothes?

Madagascar is one of the world's lowest-income countries. At first, imported clothing sounded like a luxury that should not fit.

But secondhand clothing may travel precisely because it is cheap. Used garments from wealthier countries can be compressed into large bales, graded, resold and sorted locally. What looks like a stylish secondhand shop may be the visible end of a much larger global surplus system.

This 25-minute walk did not answer where Kilobika's clothes came from, what they cost, or who buys them. It only exposed the question.

The experiment

This is not a complete explanation of shopping or poverty in Madagascar. It is a record of the path that appeared during one short online walk: craft goods, unfamiliar packaging, nearly invisible clothing shops, a secondhand store and a question about the global movement of clothes.

The photographs remain on Google Maps rather than being copied here. The next step is not to solve the mystery immediately, but to publish it and continue to the next place.
